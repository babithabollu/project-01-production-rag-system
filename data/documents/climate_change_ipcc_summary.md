# IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) — Summary for Policymakers
## Synthesized Excerpt for RAG Demo

*Source: Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change (IPCC), AR6 Synthesis Report, 2023.*
*This file is a structured summary excerpt for educational and demonstration purposes.*

## 1. Current State of the Climate

### 1.1 Observed Warming

Human activities, principally through emissions of greenhouse gases, have unequivocally caused global warming. Global surface temperature has increased by approximately **1.1°C above the 1850–1900 baseline** over the period 2011–2020. Each of the last four decades has been successively warmer than any decade that preceded it since 1850.

The rate of global warming between 1970 and 2020 is unprecedented over at least the last 2,000 years.

### 1.2 Attribution of Warming

It is unequivocal that human influence has warmed the atmosphere, ocean, and land. The primary driver is the increase in atmospheric CO2 concentration:

- Pre-industrial (1850): approximately 280 ppm CO2
- Year 2000: approximately 370 ppm CO2
- Year 2022: approximately 419 ppm CO2 — the highest level in at least 2 million years

Human-caused greenhouse gas emissions since the pre-industrial period have raised the concentrations of CO2, methane (CH4), and nitrous oxide (N2O) to levels unprecedented in the past 800,000 years.

### 1.3 Observed Impacts

- **Sea level rise**: Global mean sea level has risen by 20 cm between 1901 and 2018, with the rate of rise accelerating. Between 2006 and 2018, sea level rose at an average rate of 3.7 mm/year.
- **Ice loss**: The Greenland and Antarctic ice sheets have been losing mass since the 1990s. Arctic sea ice has declined at approximately 13% per decade since 1979.
- **Extreme weather**: The frequency and intensity of extreme heat events, heavy precipitation, droughts, and tropical cyclones has increased due to human influence.
- **Ocean acidification**: Ocean pH has decreased by 0.1 units since pre-industrial times (a 26% increase in acidity), primarily due to CO2 absorption.

## 2. Future Climate Projections

### 2.1 Temperature Scenarios

The IPCC AR6 defines five Shared Socioeconomic Pathway (SSP) scenarios for future emissions. Projected global warming by 2100:

| Scenario | Description | Warming by 2100 |
|---|---|---|
| SSP1-1.9 | Very low emissions, net-zero CO2 by 2050 | 1.0–1.8°C |
| SSP1-2.6 | Low emissions | 1.3–2.4°C |
| SSP2-4.5 | Intermediate emissions | 2.1–3.5°C |
| SSP3-7.0 | High emissions | 2.8–4.6°C |
| SSP5-8.5 | Very high emissions | 3.3–5.7°C |

Global warming is likely to exceed 1.5°C during the 21st century under all scenarios except SSP1-1.9. Warming is likely to exceed 2.0°C under SSP2-4.5, SSP3-7.0, and SSP5-8.5.

### 2.2 Sea Level Rise Projections

Under high-emission scenarios (SSP5-8.5), global mean sea level is projected to rise by:
- **0.6–1.0 meters by 2100** (likely range)
- Up to **1.5 meters or more** cannot be ruled out if ice sheet instability processes are triggered

Low-emission scenarios (SSP1-1.9) project sea level rise of 0.3–0.6 meters by 2100.

### 2.3 Extreme Events

As global warming increases, extreme heat events that occurred approximately once per 50 years in a pre-industrial climate will occur:
- Every 10 years at 1.5°C of global warming
- Every 5 years at 2°C of global warming
- Every 2 years at 4°C of global warming

Heavy rainfall events, droughts, and fire weather will also intensify with each additional degree of warming.

## 3. Carbon Budget and the 1.5°C Limit

### 3.1 Remaining Carbon Budget

The concept of a remaining carbon budget links cumulative CO2 emissions to global temperature change. As of January 2023:

- **500 Gt CO2** remains before the world likely exceeds 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels (50% probability)
- **380 Gt CO2** remains with 67% probability of staying below 1.5°C
- **1,500 Gt CO2** remaining to likely stay below 2°C

At current emission rates of approximately 40 Gt CO2 per year, the 1.5°C carbon budget would be exhausted within approximately 10–12 years.

### 3.2 Emissions Pathways

To limit warming to 1.5°C with no or limited overshoot, global CO2 emissions must:
- Decline by **43% by 2030** relative to 2019 levels
- Reach **net-zero around 2050**
- Achieve net-negative CO2 emissions in the second half of the century

Methane emissions must also be cut by approximately **34%** by 2030 to achieve 1.5°C pathways.

## 4. Mitigation Options

### 4.1 Energy System Transformation

Transitioning away from fossil fuels is the central pillar of climate mitigation. Required changes:

1. **Phase out coal**: Unabated coal power must decline by 67–82% by 2030 relative to 2019 levels
2. **Scale renewables**: Solar and wind capacity must grow 5–10x by 2030
3. **Electrify end uses**: Transportation, heating, and industrial processes must shift to electricity
4. **Improve efficiency**: Energy efficiency improvements can contribute 25–30% of mitigation potential

### 4.2 Carbon Dioxide Removal (CDR)

All pathways limiting warming to 1.5°C or 2°C involve some form of carbon dioxide removal (CDR). CDR methods include:

- **Afforestation and reforestation**: Planting forests to absorb CO2
- **Bioenergy with CCS (BECCS)**: Burning biomass for energy while capturing the CO2
- **Direct air carbon capture and storage (DACCS)**: Machines that pull CO2 directly from the air
- **Enhanced weathering**: Spreading crushed silicate rocks to accelerate natural CO2 absorption
- **Soil carbon sequestration**: Agricultural practices that store more carbon in soils

CDR at scale faces significant land, water, and biodiversity constraints. It is considered a complement to, not a substitute for, deep emissions reductions.

### 4.3 Adaptation

Even with aggressive mitigation, some degree of adaptation to climate impacts is necessary. Key adaptation strategies include:

- **Coastal protection**: Sea walls, managed retreat from flood-prone areas
- **Heat resilience**: Green infrastructure, urban cooling, early warning systems
- **Water security**: Drought-resistant crops, water recycling, watershed management
- **Ecosystem protection**: Preserving and restoring coastal wetlands, forests, and coral reefs as natural buffers

## 5. Paris Agreement

### 5.1 Key Provisions

The Paris Agreement, adopted by 196 parties in December 2015, established:

- A long-term goal to limit global average temperature increase to **well below 2°C** above pre-industrial levels, with efforts to limit it to **1.5°C**
- A requirement for all countries to submit Nationally Determined Contributions (NDCs) — national climate action plans
- A global stocktake mechanism every five years to assess collective progress
- Finance flows consistent with low-emission development

### 5.2 Current NDC Gap

Current nationally determined contributions, if fully implemented, are projected to limit warming to approximately **2.5°C** by 2100 — well above the 1.5°C target. The gap between current pledges and what is needed to meet the Paris goals is called the "ambition gap."

## 6. Tipping Points and Irreversible Changes

The IPCC AR6 identifies several potential climate tipping points — thresholds beyond which change becomes self-sustaining and difficult or impossible to reverse:

- **Collapse of Atlantic Meridional Overturning Circulation (AMOC)**: Possible with sustained warming above 4°C; would dramatically alter rainfall patterns in Europe and North America
- **Dieback of the Amazon rainforest**: Deforestation combined with drought stress could convert parts of the Amazon from carbon sink to carbon source
- **Permafrost thaw**: Arctic permafrost contains approximately 1.5 trillion tonnes of organic carbon; thawing releases methane and CO2 in a self-reinforcing feedback
- **Loss of Arctic summer sea ice**: Already observed; could become near-complete for summer periods above 1.5°C
- **Greenland ice sheet collapse**: Potentially triggered above 1.5–2°C; would contribute several meters of sea level rise over centuries

The probability and interaction of tipping points increases with each increment of warming, providing strong motivation to limit warming as much as possible.
