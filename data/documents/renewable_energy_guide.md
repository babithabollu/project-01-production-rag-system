# Renewable Energy Guide

## Overview

Renewable energy is energy derived from natural sources that replenish faster than they are consumed. Unlike fossil fuels, renewable sources produce little to no greenhouse gas emissions during operation, making them a cornerstone of strategies to address climate change.

As of 2023, renewable energy sources supply approximately 30% of global electricity generation. The International Energy Agency (IEA) projects this share could reach 60% by 2030 under accelerated transition scenarios, and up to 85% by 2050 in net-zero pathways.

---

## Solar Energy

### How Solar Photovoltaic (PV) Works

Solar photovoltaic cells convert sunlight directly into electricity through the photovoltaic effect. When photons from sunlight strike a semiconductor material — typically crystalline silicon — they knock electrons loose from their atoms, creating a flow of electric current. A single solar cell produces a small amount of direct current (DC); panels group many cells together, and inverters convert DC to alternating current (AC) for household and grid use.

### Cost Decline

The cost of utility-scale solar PV has fallen by more than 89% between 2010 and 2022, from approximately $0.38/kWh to $0.049/kWh. This makes solar the cheapest source of new electricity generation in history in most regions of the world.

### Capacity Factor

Solar panels typically achieve a capacity factor of 10–25%, meaning they generate electricity at 10–25% of their theoretical maximum output over a year. Capacity factors vary with latitude, cloud cover, and panel orientation. Utility-scale solar farms in high-irradiance regions like the Middle East and southwestern United States can reach 25–30%.

### Global Installed Capacity

Global solar PV installed capacity reached 1.2 terawatts (TW) in 2022, with China accounting for approximately 35% of global capacity. The United States, European Union, India, and Japan are also major markets.

## Wind Energy

### How Wind Turbines Work

Wind turbines convert the kinetic energy of moving air into mechanical energy, which drives a generator to produce electricity. Modern horizontal-axis turbines use aerodynamic blades that generate lift as wind flows over them — similar to airplane wings. This rotational force spins the rotor, which is connected either directly or via a gearbox to the generator.

### Onshore vs Offshore Wind

**Onshore wind** turbines are installed on land and have a capacity factor of 25–40%, depending on wind resources. They are the most cost-competitive source of new electricity in many regions.

**Offshore wind** turbines are installed at sea, where winds are stronger and more consistent. Offshore wind achieves capacity factors of 40–55% and can be sited near coastal population centers. The cost of offshore wind has declined by approximately 60% since 2010.

### Global Installed Capacity

Total wind capacity reached 899 GW globally in 2022. China leads with approximately 365 GW of installed capacity, followed by the United States (140 GW) and Germany (66 GW).

## Hydropower

### How Hydropower Works

Hydropower generates electricity by directing water flow through turbines. Conventional hydropower dams create a reservoir; when water is released through penstocks, it drives turbines connected to generators. Run-of-river plants divert a portion of river flow without large reservoirs.

### Scale and Share

Hydropower is the largest source of renewable electricity globally, providing approximately 16% of world electricity production and over 60% of all renewable electricity. Global installed hydropower capacity exceeds 1,300 GW.

### Pumped Storage

Pumped hydropower storage (PHS) accounts for over 90% of all utility-scale energy storage capacity worldwide. During periods of low electricity demand, excess power pumps water from a lower reservoir to an upper reservoir. During high demand, water flows back down through turbines to generate electricity.

## Geothermal Energy

### How Geothermal Works

Geothermal energy harnesses heat from the Earth's interior. In high-temperature geothermal systems, steam or hot water from underground reservoirs drives turbines to generate electricity. Low-temperature geothermal systems provide direct heating for buildings, greenhouses, and industrial processes.

### Where It Is Used

Geothermal is used extensively in Iceland (which meets ~66% of primary energy needs from geothermal), the United States (3.7 GW installed capacity, primarily in California and Nevada), Kenya (where geothermal supplies ~47% of electricity), and the Philippines, Indonesia, and New Zealand.

## Energy Storage

### The Intermittency Challenge

Solar and wind energy are variable — they generate electricity only when the sun shines or wind blows. Matching supply with demand requires energy storage or flexible backup generation.

### Battery Storage

Lithium-ion battery storage costs have fallen by over 90% since 2010, from approximately $1,200/kWh to below $130/kWh in 2022. Grid-scale battery installations exceeded 45 GW globally in 2023.

Battery storage addresses short-duration needs (minutes to hours), enabling grid operators to smooth fluctuations in solar and wind output.

### Long-Duration Storage

For multi-day or seasonal storage, technologies under development include iron-air batteries, flow batteries, compressed air energy storage (CAES), and green hydrogen (electrolysis using renewable electricity to split water into hydrogen and oxygen).

## Renewable Energy and Emissions Reduction

The electricity sector accounts for approximately 34% of global CO2 emissions. Transitioning power generation from fossil fuels to renewables is the single largest lever for reducing greenhouse gas emissions.

The IPCC Sixth Assessment Report (AR6) identifies rapid scaling of renewable energy as essential to limiting global warming to 1.5°C above pre-industrial levels. Under net-zero scenarios, global solar capacity must reach 14–18 TW by 2050, and wind capacity must reach 8–10 TW — representing growth of approximately 10–15x current installed capacity.

## Key Terms

- **Capacity factor**: Ratio of actual energy output to maximum possible output over a period.
- **Levelized cost of energy (LCOE)**: All-in cost per unit of electricity over a plant's lifetime.
- **Photovoltaic effect**: Generation of electric current when light strikes a semiconductor.
- **Intermittency**: Variability of energy output from solar and wind sources.
- **Grid parity**: Point where renewable electricity costs equal or undercut fossil fuel electricity costs.
